Chapter 1: Arrival

The Meridian had been quiet for fourteen months when the signal arrived. [first-contact] [sagan-1985]

Wide establishing shot: the Meridian drifts through unbroken black. A single amber light pulses on the comms panel. No one is watching.

Yusuf noticed it during handoff. He almost didn't — the log entry sat between a routine magnetosphere reading and a reminder about the water recycler — but the frequency stopped him. Hydrogen line. Clean. Structured.

He stared at it for a long moment, then called the captain.

The hydrogen line sits at 1420 MHz, the most abundant frequency in the universe. It was proposed as the logical channel for interstellar communication in 1959 by Cocconi and Morrison, on the grounds that any technological civilization would know to look there. The WOW! signal of 1977 arrived at exactly this frequency and has never been explained. [wow-signal-1977</aside>]

Nova read the data without speaking. She read it again. Then she pulled up the analysis suite and ran the numbers herself, not because she doubted Yusuf but because she needed the minute it took.

Structured. Repeating. Not a pulsar. Not a quasar. Not anything she could explain away.

"Wake the others," she said.

The crew assembles in the comms bay. Six people around a small table. The readout glows on the wall behind Nova. Nobody speaks first.

The argument took three hours. [captain-nova]

Reyes wanted to respond immediately — we didn't come this far to be polite about it — and Okonkwo wanted to wait for peer review, which would take years of light-speed relay and would almost certainly produce no consensus. Chen said nothing, which meant she agreed with Okonkwo. Park said nothing, which meant he didn't know. Yusuf, who had found it, said he didn't feel like it was his call to make.

Nova listened to all of it.

A response sent at light speed takes 4.2 years to reach the nearest star system. A reply takes another 4.2 years to return. The decision to transmit is, for all practical purposes, a decision made on behalf of people not yet born.

When the room went quiet, she asked each of them the same question: If it were your call alone, what would you do?

Reyes said yes. Okonkwo said wait. Chen said wait. Park said he genuinely didn't know. Yusuf looked at his hands.

"Thank you," Nova said. "Get some sleep."

She sat alone in the comms bay for forty minutes.

The signal played on loop. Structured. Patient. Fourteen months from home, and something had been waiting here, broadcasting on the one frequency that said I know you'll be listening.

At 0340 ship time, she composed a response. Six words — the prime sequence, the hydrogen frequency, a coordinate echo — and sent it.

The transmission log updated.

SENT 0341:07 — TX_001 — OUTBOUND

No take-backs.

The Meridian, unchanged, drifting. The amber light still pulses on the comms panel. The transmission is already gone, moving at the speed of light toward something that may or may not be listening.
